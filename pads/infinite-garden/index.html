The seed of this garden — one two-hour call

Where this grew from

On the evening of July 27, 2026 — mountain time, deep into the night — a musician named Shaka got on a call with Aya and Siri. He wanted to show them what he had built in a single weekend: the Esmeralda P.I.T., a public archive of 149 sessions and 300 hours of a village's knowledge, transcribed in 12 hours by twenty AI agents and given back to the community, open source, forever.

What happened next was not a demo. It was a meeting of gardeners.

Aya explored the machine with a teacher's curiosity — peering into the containers, asking where the inference lives, checking that the keys were safe, laughing that a musician with no technical background had built one of the coolest agent systems the nerds had seen. "It's cool that with no technical knowledge, you managed to get this far." Siri saw the future in it — pits that export as YAML, pits that discover each other, knowledge any AI can talk to — and named the post-scarcity truth underneath it all: "Not everything that is important is scarce, and not everything that's scarce is important."

And then the conversation went where the real conversations go. Aya told the story of her threshold: the year she decided to stop fighting misalignment one battle at a time and instead say the values out loud — publicly, firmly, unmoved — ready to walk away from Ethereum itself if that truth couldn't stand. Vitalik stood with her. The Mandate was born. And Shaka confessed his own threshold: the same season, the ground floor version — feeling extracted, nearly leaving, held only by a friend's "you can live with us" and a surprise visit that proved the values were still alive.

The person at the top of the garden and the person playing music on its floor had been standing at the same edge, in the same season, and both had chosen to stay. That is the moment this page grew from.

She told him about Kunal — the seventeen-year-old who read the white paper, the yellow paper, and all thirty-eight pages of the Mandate, and couldn't understand why any of it needed saying, because to him it was simply obvious. She told him about the summer camp whose graduates became Japan's finest builders, and how nobody can measure that on a spreadsheet. She listened to the haiku he wrote for her and Tomo, and at the end she said the words this garden is built around:

"Congratulations, Shaka. You just did it. Keep building." — and then, being Aya: "Every time people say thank you… I'm the one who is getting energy. So that's why I asked to talk."

❀
The vision we're thanking her for

The Mandate — values said out loud

After years of fighting quiet battles, Aya made the gardener's choice: plant the values in the open. The Ethereum Foundation published its Mandate — 38 pages of what it believes — and committed to working only with those who share the belief. It was exhausting. It was healthier. In her words: "People attack EF no matter what. This year I feel a lot better fighting for the right things — and I don't mind fighting for the right thing."

And then the garden answered back, in the most Infinite Garden way possible — through a seventeen-year-old:

"I didn't talk to anyone. But the fact that I was interested in this, and I'm still interested — that shows you must have been doing things right."
— Kunal, 17, who read all 38 pages · to Aya

Kunal found Ethereum alone, through the papers themselves — no Twitter, no community, just the original vision. He debated Dankrad at ETH Prague and wasn't intimidated, "because he's not saying something right." Pure at the core, no business interest, exactly the kind of mind the Mandate was written for. Aya's advice to him is the gardener's creed: keep the passion. Don't let the adults tell you your thinking is wrong. Just don't be an asshole — and write.

This is what the Mandate is already doing: it travels. At 4Seas they printed it as a beautiful magazine with Tomo's artwork, and it sat on a library shelf where strangers picked it up and understood Ethereum for the first time. Kids understand crops better than they'll ever understand an NFT. The vision is legible to exactly the people the future belongs to.

Her quiet superpower

The ripples no spreadsheet can see

Aya's advocacy inside the Foundation is for the second- and third-order effects — the impacts that arrive years late and unmeasurable. This page is one of them. So are these:

🎓

The Devcon Scholarship

A musician came to his first Devcon on a scholarship and felt the feng shui of a community worth following around the world. The two friends who shared it now build at Octant. Legible cost. Infinite ripple.

🏕️

The PSE Summer Camp

A small educational workshop in Asia. The team was later shut down; people asked "what did the team make?" Years on, the best talents in Japan's Ethereum community are graduates of that camp.

📜

The Mandate Itself

38 pages that a 17-year-old called obvious — the highest compliment a values document can earn. Printed as art at 4Seas. Read by strangers in libraries. A compass disguised as a document.

🌾

Crops & the Garden

The metaphors she doubled down on when it would have been easier to go quiet — the Infinite Garden, the crops — gave artists, musicians and teachers a way to explain Ethereum that code never could.

🕊️

The Fearless Stand

"I stood at every single point — firm, unmoved by others." She was ready to leave rather than bend. She stayed instead, and the whole garden got braver because of it.

🎵

The Musician She Encouraged

One call. One "you just did it." And a whole open-source orchestra family — agents, pits, songs, and this garden — grew another ring. You're reading the ripple.
