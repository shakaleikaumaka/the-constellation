The Whole System at a Glance

read me first
  • The stall: two 275-gal IBC totes, tops cut out, stacked open-top-to-open-top = one tall stall you stand up in. Cages stay fully intact.
  • The basin: the stall sits on top of two galvanized cattle troughs — every drop lands in steel, never on playa.
  • The drain: a float switch wakes a little pump that pushes greywater to a storage tote. Automatic.
  • This is the locked design from the David syncs (7/2 + 7/16). Section 1 of the stall is already built. 💪
David: "How you do the containment on a shower is the hardest part of the whole project." That's why the troughs and the pump get their own steps — the stall is the easy part.

Cut the Totes

done for set 1 ✅ — recipe for the next camp
  • Cut 1: hot knife / jigsaw the bladder top out of both totes. Steel cage untouched — it's the building.
  • Cut 2: doorway in the side of the bottom tote, with a threshold left at the bottom so water stays in the pan.
  • Every cut edge: deburr at 45°, sand with 120-grit, cap with split foam pipe insulation or split hose, zip-tied through the cage.
⚠ The one injury this build can cause is a hip or shoulder on a raw HDPE edge in a wet stall. Cap every edge, then tug-test each cap. No exceptions.
4

Stack & Join the Cages

2 people · section 1 done ✅
  • Flip tote 2 upside-down (pallet becomes the roof) and lower it onto tote 1 — open top to open top.
  • Line the cage rims up flush, then bolt or wire the rims together at every rail crossing. The joint should feel like one rigid tower.
  • Result: a ~7½-ft standing-height stall with built-in walls, a roof frame to hang from, and a doorway.
David's rule: the intact steel cage is the entire structural system — the plastic alone would never stand up to playa wind. That's why we never cut steel, only plastic.
5

Set It on the Troughs

the 7/16 design 🔄
  • Two galvanized troughs side by side on packed, level ground; the stall rests on top of their rims.
  • Each trough is at least 10" bigger than the stall footprint, and at least one runs ~2 ft longer — that open end is the service bay: pump access, dish-tray dumping, greywater pours.
  • Everything that drips in the stall lands in steel and flows to the pump. Zero playa contact.
  • Strap the cage to staked anchors — it's a sail when it's empty.
⚠ Honest open item: exactly how the stall seats + anchors on the trough rims (spacers? saddle brackets?) is still being finalized by David + Jarrett. Don't improvise this joint — ask first. 🐢
6

The Float Switch — Build 3

Jeremy fabricating · then PRACTICE
  • Bolt each float switch to its own weight: 2"×2" angle iron, ~6" long, ≥3/16" wall, #10 screws. It sits on the trough floor and can't wander.
  • Never attach the float to the pump — separate, self-weighted unit (David was emphatic).
  • Power passes through the float switch: wall → float plug → pump plugs into its back. The float is the pump's on/off brain.
  • Make 3: shower trough, kitchen-sink basin, one spare. Jeremy has the fabrication.
David: float switches are "magic" — but only if you've played with them. Bucket of water, float, pump: run the cycle until it's boring. Do this at the Gulch, not on playa.
7

Pump & Greywater Plumbing

1 hr once parts arrive
  • The submersible utility pump sits at the trough's low point in the service bay; the float unit sits beside it.
  • Waste line = garden hose marked WASTE — twin 50-ft lengths beat one 100 (source sits close to the road tank). Spring-clip the hose to the cage so it can't whip when the pump kicks.
  • Greywater goes to the storage tote by the access road, where the service truck pumps it out. Never onto playa.
  • Power: GFCI circuit, cord exits high and dry, connections never in the splash zone.
⚠ Water + 120V is the one place we don't improvise. If a cord, plug, or splice would sit low or wet — stop and re-route. Questions go to David or Jarrett.
8

Water In — the Solar Bag

45 min rigging
  • Pulley (or smooth carabiner, 50-lb+) on the top cage rail, 1/4" rope down to a cleat at waist height — anyone hoists their own bag.
  • Backup clip: bag handle clips to the top rail too, so full weight never rides the hoist line alone.
  • The bag's shut-off head is the whole ethos: wet down → OFF → soap → rinse.
  • 1–2 gal per shower keeps the greywater volume tiny and the queue moving. 💧
Later upgrade (maybe): a pumped line from the potable system can fill bags at the stall. Bags first — it already works with zero plumbing.
9

Privacy & Comfort

45 min · the human layer
  • Curtain across the doorway on a rod or taut rope; the other three sides are already opaque tote walls.
  • Hooks high on the cage outside the splash zone; a grippy mat inside; a clip-on light for night showers.
  • A small chalkboard — "bags warm: 3" — turns solar timing into a self-organizing queue.

🔥 Hot water, honestly

☀ Sun-warmed bag (the default): a black solar bag in full afternoon sun gets genuinely hot for free. Golden-hour showers are warm showers. This is the plan.

🫖 Kettle mix: chilly morning? Pour a kitchen kettle of hot water into the bag with cool water until it's shower-warm to the wrist. Simple, safe, works.

🚫 Propane tankless heaters exist, but David called that "a very difficult system" — gas + water + wind is specialist territory. If the camp ever wants one, that's a David conversation, not a solo project.

10

Test Day — the Greywater Drill

1 hr at the Gulch · before the playa!
  • Full dress rehearsal at the Gulch: pour water, watch the float trip, watch the pump run to dryness and shut off.
  • Then a real shower rehearsal — bag, curtain, threshold step, pump-out — while someone walks the perimeter looking for escapees.
  • Any drip that leaves the trough = fix now, not on playa. Leave No Trace starts working here.
  • Drag a towel along every capped edge — a snag means re-cap.
David's homework, standing order: get every piece in your hands and physically mess with it before the playa. Practiced systems work; imagined systems don't.
